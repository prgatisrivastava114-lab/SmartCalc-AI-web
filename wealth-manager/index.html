What a wealth manager does

Wealth management usually means handling a larger portfolio end to end — deciding the investment approach, implementing it, watching the tax position, and coordinating with your other advisers. It tends to suit people whose holdings have become complex enough that keeping track of them is a job in itself.

  • Portfolio construction — an allocation built around your goals and timeframes.
  • Implementation — choosing and rebalancing the actual holdings.
  • Tax coordination — managing how gains are realised across the year.
  • Consolidated reporting — one view across accounts rather than scattered statements.
  • Succession and estate support — nominations, joint holdings and will coordination.

When it is worth it — and when it is not

Wealth management earns its cost when your holdings are diverse, your tax position is complicated, or you simply have neither the time nor the inclination to manage it yourself. It is hard to justify if your situation is straightforward — a few funds and a retirement account, reviewed once a year, is genuinely manageable on your own.

  • Worth considering if — you hold multiple property, business or equity positions; you have cross-border or estate considerations; or your portfolio has grown past the point where you can track it comfortably.
  • Probably not needed if — your assets are simple, your goals are few, and you are comfortable reviewing them annually.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between a wealth manager and a financial planner?

A financial planner builds the overall plan — goals, insurance, tax, retirement and investments together. A wealth manager generally focuses on managing a larger investment portfolio and may coordinate tax and estate matters. Many people use both, or a single firm that offers both services. We can connect you with a Certified Financial Planner for the planning side, or you can look at options near you.

Is there a minimum portfolio size for wealth management?

Often there is, and it varies by firm — some work with portfolios of a certain size and above, others have no formal minimum. Ask directly. If you are below the threshold, a fee-only adviser or a self-managed plan may serve you just as well.

How are wealth managers paid?

Usually a percentage of the assets they manage, sometimes with a separate planning fee, and sometimes with commission on products. Ask which applies, what the percentage is, and whether it changes as the portfolio grows.

Do I need a wealth manager to build wealth?

No. The biggest drivers of wealth for most families are unglamorous: saving regularly, keeping costs low, staying invested, carrying enough insurance, and not selling in a panic. A manager helps with the administration and discipline; the behaviour is still yours.

What should I check before handing over a portfolio?

Ask who holds the assets and who can move them, what the total cost is including fund charges, how often you will receive reports, and what happens if you want to leave. Your money should remain in your own name with a regulated custodian, not with the manager.
