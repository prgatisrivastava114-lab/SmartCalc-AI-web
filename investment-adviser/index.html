Advice and selling are not the same thing

An adviser is supposed to look at your situation and recommend what suits it. Someone selling a product is paid when you buy that product. Both can be perfectly honest, but you should always know which conversation you are in. The simplest way to find out is to ask how the person is paid, and to ask for it in writing.

  • Fee-only — you pay them directly; they receive nothing from product providers.
  • Commission-based — they are paid by the company whose product you buy.
  • Both — a fee for the plan, plus commission on what you buy. This exists, and it is worth asking about explicitly.

What good investment advice looks like

  • It starts with your goals and timeframes — money you need in three years should not be invested like money you need in twenty.
  • It matches risk to your situation — your ability to tolerate a fall depends on your income stability and how soon you need the money.
  • It is diversified — spreading across asset types and within them, rather than concentrating in whatever has performed best recently.
  • It is cost-aware — the charges you pay reduce your return, and small differences compound over decades.
  • It is reviewed — allocation drifts as markets move and as your life changes.

What to prepare

Questions people ask

What does an investment adviser do?

An investment adviser helps you decide how to invest — typically asset allocation, fund or portfolio selection, and periodic review. That is narrower than a full financial plan, which also covers insurance, tax, retirement and estate matters.

Should I see an adviser or make a full financial plan first?

Plan first. The plan tells you how much money needs to go where and by when; only then does it make sense to discuss which investments to use. Doing it the other way round is how people end up with products that do not match their goals.

How much does investment advice cost?

It varies — some advisers charge a percentage of the amount invested, some a flat or hourly fee, and some are paid by commission from product providers. Ask for the structure and the figure in writing before you agree to anything.

What is the difference between an adviser and a wealth manager?

Wealth managers generally work with larger portfolios and often handle more of the implementation, including tax and estate coordination. An investment adviser may serve a wider range of portfolio sizes. The right choice usually depends on the size and complexity of what you hold.

Can I manage my own investments instead?

Yes, and many people do successfully — especially with a simple, diversified, low-cost approach and a once-a-year review. The main things self-managing investors miss are an honest check on insurance cover and a realistic inflation-adjusted goal calculation, both of which you can do here for free.
