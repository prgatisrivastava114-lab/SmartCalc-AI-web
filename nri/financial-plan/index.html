About this planner

Most financial plans assume you earn, save and spend in one currency, and that the tax system you live under is the one you will retire under. For an NRI neither is true. You earn in dirhams or dollars, a chunk of your money sits in India earning rupee returns, your children may study in a third country, and the day you move back changes which of your income the Indian system can reach.

This tool plans around those three facts. It is not a tax return and it is not advice. It is the arithmetic of a cross-border family budget, written out so you can see where it breaks.

How this planner works

  1. Each goal is priced where it will be spent. A goal tagged abroad inflates at the inflation rate where you live and is converted to rupees at the rate you enter. A goal tagged India inflates at Indian inflation. Nothing is averaged.
  2. Retirement is solved as a drawdown, not a round number. The corpus is sized so it is exhausted exactly at the age you plan until, using the real return — the inflation-adjusted return — rather than the headline one.
  3. Your existing money and your monthly saving are grown forward at the accumulation rate to the year you retire, and the shortfall is whatever the requirement exceeds.
  4. The shortfall is converted into a monthly investment using the same annuity relation, and shown in rupees and in your own currency.
  5. Then the two NRI-only questions are answered: how long your RNOR shelter is likely to last if you return, and how many years it would take to move your Indian corpus out under the FEMA remittance cap.

The formula

The real return — the Fisher relation, not a subtraction — drives everything:

real return  r* = (r − i) ÷ (1 + i)

A corpus that must pay a monthly sum W for D years and finish at zero:

corpus = W × 12 × (1 − (1 + r*)−D) ÷ r*

What your money becomes, and the monthly investment that closes a gap:

future value = L × (1 + r)y + S × ((1 + m)k − 1) ÷ m × (1 + m)
monthly need = gap × m ÷ (((1 + m)k − 1) × (1 + m))

L is what you hold today, S the monthly investment, m the monthly rate, k the number of months. Using r − i in place of the real return overstates a 25-year corpus by several per cent, which is why it is written out above.

A worked example

Take the figures the page loads with: 42 years old, in the UAE for eight years, saving AED 3,500 a month, AED 150,000 invested abroad and ₹30 lakh in India, retiring at 58 in India on ₹1,20,000 a month in today's money, with a child's university abroad at AED 60,000 in twelve years, a home in India at ₹90 lakh in six, and ₹25 lakh for parents in three.

That family is not on track, and the page says so rather than reassuring them. The retirement line alone needs a corpus in the region of ₹8.5 crore, because ₹1,20,000 a month today becomes roughly ₹3 lakh a month after sixteen years of 6% inflation, and it has to last twenty-seven years. The university goal is the one that behaves differently: priced in dirhams and inflated at 2.5% rather than 6%, it grows far more slowly than an Indian goal of the same rupee size would — which is exactly the distinction a single-currency planner hides.

The currency-match panel is usually the surprise. A family with most of its assets abroad but almost all of its goals in India is carrying a bet on the exchange rate that nobody chose deliberately.

Assumptions and limitations

  • The exchange rate is held flat. This is the single biggest simplification. We do not forecast currencies — nobody can — so today's rate is applied to every future year. Over twenty years that will be wrong. The currency-match panel exists so you can see how exposed you are to its being wrong.
  • The starting rates are a convenience, not a quote. They are rounded, and they are not live. Overwrite them with the rate your bank actually gives you.
  • Returns are assumed steady. Real markets deliver the same average in a different order, and a bad first decade in retirement does more damage than the same bad decade later. No sequence-of-returns risk is modelled.
  • Tax is not deducted from returns. Treat the output as pre-tax.
  • The RNOR window is an estimate from one input. The real test turns on day counts across the previous seven and ten years. Use the residential status calculator, then confirm with a chartered accountant before you act on it.
  • The repatriation figure assumes the whole Indian corpus moves through the NRO route. Money already in NRE or FCNR is freely repatriable and does not consume the limit.
  • Nothing here is tax advice. Treaty positions, documentation and your own facts decide the outcome.

Frequently asked questions

Why does the currency a goal is spent in matter so much?

Because it changes two things at once: the inflation rate that grows the cost, and the exchange rate that converts it. A goal in India typically inflates around 6% a year; one in the Gulf, Europe or North America usually inflates far less. Pricing an overseas university at Indian inflation overstates it badly, and pricing an Indian wedding at overseas inflation understates it. The second effect is the exchange rate — if your money is in one currency and your goal is in another, a rate move changes what you can afford even though nothing about your investments changed.

Does the tool forecast the exchange rate?

No, and you should be wary of anything that claims to. Today’s rate is held flat across every future year. That is certainly wrong over a twenty-year plan, which is why the currency-match panel shows how much of your plan is exposed to it rather than pretending to a forecast.

Is the RNOR window reliable?

It is an estimate from a single input — how long you have been non-resident. The actual test depends on your day counts over the preceding seven and ten financial years. Use it to understand whether a return window is worth planning around, then settle your real position with the residential status calculator and a chartered accountant before you move money or sell anything.

Why would it take years to bring my own money home?

Under the FEMA remittance rules a person resident outside India may remit up to USD 1 million per financial year from an NRO account, in addition to current income. For most families that is irrelevant. For a family with a large Indian corpus, a property sale or an inheritance, the cap — not the market — decides the timetable, and it is better known early than discovered at the point of sale.

Can I use this if I am not planning to return to India?

Yes. Set the return question to No and the RNOR panel steps aside. The currency match, the goal maths and the repatriation runway all still apply, and the mismatch question is usually sharper for someone settling abroad permanently while still holding Indian assets.
