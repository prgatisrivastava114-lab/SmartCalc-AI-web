Retiring in India on foreign earnings

Almost every retirement calculator assumes you earn, save and spend in one currency, at one inflation rate. If you are an NRI planning to retire in India, none of those assumptions hold — and the error does not cancel out. It compounds.

The mistake that makes the number wrong

A retirement target is just today's monthly spending, grown by inflation to the year you retire, then multiplied by enough years of drawdown. The arithmetic is simple. The assumption underneath it is where NRI plans go wrong.

A general-purpose Indian calculator applies one inflation rate to all your spending, because it assumes you live where you will retire. An NRI usually does not. Until you move back, your school fees, rent and groceries inflate at the rate of the country you live in — and those rates differ substantially from India's.

Why the error does not wash out. Inflation compounds. Applying a higher rate to spending that is not actually subject to it does not produce a slightly wrong answer over a long horizon; it produces a different answer. The longer your runway, the wider the gap — which is the opposite of what you want from a planning tool.

The planner on this site therefore asks where each goal will be spent, not merely what it costs, and inflates each one at the rate of its own country.

Real return is not return minus inflation

The second error is quieter and appears in a great many published calculators. To work out what a corpus must be worth in today's money, you need the real rate of return — what your money earns after inflation has been taken out. Most tools subtract:

real return ≈ nominal return − inflation  —  this is an approximation, and it is wrong at the long horizons retirement planning uses

The correct relation, the Fisher equation, divides rather than subtracts:

Real return(1 + r) ÷ (1 + i) − 1
rnominal return, as a decimal
iinflation, as a decimal

Subtraction overstates the real return slightly every year, and because the drawdown formula discounts over decades, a small overstatement becomes a materially smaller corpus. A plan built on the approximation quietly tells you that you need less money than you do.

How the target is actually built

Four steps, in order. Each one is a formula you can reproduce in a spreadsheet.

StepWhat it does
1. Inflate the spendingTake today's monthly spend and grow it to your retirement year at the inflation rate of the country where that money will be spent.
2. Find the real returnApply the Fisher relation above to your expected post-retirement return. This is the rate your corpus earns in purchasing-power terms while you draw it down.
3. Size the corpusA present-value annuity over your drawdown years: the lump sum that funds that inflated monthly spend for as long as you expect to need it.
4. Close the gapProject what your existing assets and current saving will become, subtract it from the target, and convert the shortfall into a monthly contribution.

The planner shows each of these as a separate figure rather than one headline number, because a plan you cannot check is a plan you cannot trust.

The exchange rate: what can and cannot be assumed

If you earn in dirhams, pounds or dollars and will spend in rupees, your plan contains a currency assumption whether you state one or not. Two honest positions:

  • You cannot forecast it. Nobody can reliably predict a currency pair over twenty years. Any tool that quietly embeds an appreciation trend is making a forecast on your behalf.
  • You should still state it. Assuming a rate of exactly today's, forever, is also a forecast — just an unexamined one.

The planner takes the middle position: it seeds a conversion rate, shows it to you, and lets you change it. The seeded rates are starting points, not live quotes, and they are deliberately not fetched from a market feed — a retirement plan that silently changes every morning is not a plan. Run your number at a rate you consider conservative, then run it again at one you consider optimistic, and look at the distance between them. That distance is your currency risk.

Three things that make an NRI plan different

Your return date changes your tax, not just your geography

Residency is tested per financial year, and the year runs 1 April to 31 March. Moving back in one half of the year rather than the other can place you in a different status for that year, which affects what is taxable while you are drawing down. Check your status →

There is usually a transitional window

A long-term NRI returning to India is usually RNOR for about two to three financial years. It is finite, it does not repeat, and the planning you do inside it is not the planning you do afterwards. What RNOR means →

Bringing the money home has its own rules

A plan that assumes a foreign corpus can simply arrive in India on the day you retire is incomplete. Repatriation has limits and a process. How repatriation works →

What this approach does not do

  • It does not forecast currencies, markets or inflation. You supply the assumptions; it applies them consistently.
  • It does not model the tax rules of the country you currently live in. Those matter and this site cannot see them.
  • It does not know your visa, pension entitlements or social-security position abroad.
  • It assumes you keep contributing as stated. Interruptions change the answer.
  • It is an estimate from your inputs, not a recommendation to buy or sell anything.
Run it, then take it to a professional. The value of doing the arithmetic yourself first is that you arrive at a conversation knowing which assumptions your plan is most sensitive to. That is a better use of a chartered accountant's time than asking them to start from nothing.

Frequently asked questions

How much do I need to retire in India as an NRI?

There is no single figure, because it depends on your monthly spending, how many years away retirement is, how long you expect the money to last, and what return you assume. The method is fixed even though the answer is not: inflate today’s spending to your retirement year, convert the post-retirement return into a real return using the Fisher relation, then size a corpus that funds that spending for your drawdown period. The planner on this site runs exactly those steps and shows each figure separately.

Can I just use a normal Indian retirement calculator?

You can, but it will apply one inflation rate to everything and assume a single currency. If you are spending abroad until you move back, that overstates the cost of your foreign spending and the error compounds over the horizon. It also typically uses return-minus-inflation rather than the Fisher relation, which understates the corpus you need.

What exchange rate should I assume?

One you can defend, and then a second one to test it. Nobody can forecast a currency pair over a retirement horizon, so the honest approach is to state the assumption openly and see how much your plan moves when you change it. The planner seeds a rate you can edit; those seeds are starting points, not live market quotes.

Does it matter which month I move back to India?

It can. Residential status is tested per financial year against day counts, so the same decision to return can produce a different status depending on where in the year you arrive. That affects what is taxable in that year. It is worth checking your position before fixing travel dates rather than after.

Should I keep saving abroad or start investing in India?

That depends on your tax position in both countries, your repatriation plans, your currency view and your time horizon — none of which a calculator can see. What this site can do is show you the size of the gap and what it takes to close it. The allocation decision belongs in a conversation with a qualified professional who knows both sides.
