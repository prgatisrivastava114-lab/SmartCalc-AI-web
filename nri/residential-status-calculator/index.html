Are you NRI, RNOR or resident?

Indian tax follows your days in India, not your passport or your visa. This runs the day-count tests in order and shows you which one decided your answer — including the two that simpler calculators leave out.

How that was decided

    About this calculator

    Residency is the question everything else hangs on. It decides whether India taxes only what you earn here or everything you earn anywhere. It is settled year by year, on physical presence, over the Indian financial year (1 April to 31 March).

    Two rules get missed by simpler tools, and both can flip an answer: the 120-day threshold for visitors with higher Indian income, and deemed residency, which can apply at zero days in India.

    How this calculator works

    1. Primary test. 182 days or more in India during the financial year ⇒ resident.
    2. Secondary test. 60 days or more this year AND 365 days or more across the preceding four years ⇒ resident.
    3. Adjust the 60-day limb for your situation, which is where most of the nuance lives.
    4. Check deemed residency for Indian citizens with higher Indian income who pay tax nowhere.
    5. If resident, grade it — RNOR or ordinarily resident.

    How the 60-day limb changes

    Who you areWhat changes
    Indian citizen leaving India for employment abroad, or crew of an Indian shipthe 60-day limb is replaced by 182 days
    Indian citizen or PIO visiting India, Indian-source income ₹15 lakh or lessthe 60-day limb is replaced by 182 days
    Indian citizen or PIO visiting India, Indian-source income above ₹15 lakhthe 60-day limb is replaced by 120 days

    Counts Indian-source income only. Foreign salary, foreign interest and overseas rent are excluded from this ₹15 lakh test.

    Deemed residency

    An Indian citizen whose Indian-source income exceeds ₹15 lakh and who is not liable to tax in any other country is treated as a resident, and specifically as RNOR.

    This can apply with zero days spent in India. It is aimed at citizens in zero-tax jurisdictions. It does not apply if you pay tax somewhere abroad. Indian citizens only — not OCI cardholders or foreign nationals of Indian origin.

    RNOR

    A resident is RNOR (Resident but Not Ordinarily Resident) if they were non-resident in 9 of the 10 preceding years, OR were in India for 729 days or fewer across the preceding 7 years.

    A person who is resident only because of the 120-day limb, or under deemed residency, is RNOR by operation of law. A long-term NRI returning to India is usually RNOR for about two to three financial years.

    Counting days correctly

    • Both travel days count. The day you land and the day you leave are each a day in India. A long weekend is not zero days.
    • The year is 1 April to 31 March — not the calendar year, and not your home country's tax year.
    • Every visit counts, however short. Several brief trips add up the same as one long stay.
    • Keep the evidence. Entry and exit records are what the department accepts. Reconstruct them while they are easy to obtain, not when a question arrives.

    The tests, written out

    The law applies these in order, and so does the calculator. The first one that fires decides the answer.

    Resident IF days_this_year ≥ 182

    Resident IF days_this_year ≥ limb AND days_prior_4_years ≥ 365

    limb = 182 if leaving for employment or crew = 182 if visiting citizen/PIO and Indian income ≤ ₹15,00,000 = 120 if visiting citizen/PIO and Indian income > ₹15,00,000 = 60 otherwise

    Deemed resident (RNOR) IF Indian citizen AND Indian income > ₹15,00,000 AND not liable to tax anywhere

    If resident: RNOR IF non_resident_in ≥ 9 of last 10 years OR days_prior_7_years ≤ 729 OR resident only via the 120-day limb OR deemed resident otherwise ROR

    Three worked examples

    An engineer in Dubai, 40 days in India

    Indian income of about ₹6,00,000 from a let-out flat, so at or below the ₹15,00,000 line. As a visiting citizen the 60-day limb lifts to 182. Forty days is below both 182 and the lifted limb, so neither test fires. Result: Non-Resident. The Dubai salary is not taxed in India.

    A consultant with ₹18,00,000 of Indian income, 130 days in India

    Indian income is above the threshold, so the limb is 120 rather than 182. Days are 130 — below 182, so the primary test does not fire, but at or above the 120-day limb. With 365 days or more across the preceding four years, the secondary test fires. Result: RNOR. The Indian income is taxable as it always would be, but foreign income stays outside the Indian net because residency arrived through the 120-day limb.

    An engineer moving home for good in May

    More than 300 days in India that year, so the primary test fires immediately and she is resident. She was non-resident in each of the previous ten years, which satisfies the 9-of-10 test. Result: RNOR — and it will typically hold for two to three years before she becomes ordinarily resident.

    Assumptions and limitations

    • This runs the day-count tests only. Residency can also be affected by a treaty tie-breaker where two countries both claim you.
    • The result is only as good as your day counts. If you are unsure, reconstruct them from travel records before relying on the answer.
    • Short stays for specified purposes can carry their own treatment in particular cases.
    • Status is decided separately for each financial year. Last year's answer does not carry over.
    • The FEMA definition of an NRI, which governs your bank accounts, is a different test from this one.

    Frequently asked questions

    What is the 120-day rule?

    For an Indian citizen or PIO who lives abroad and visits India, the usual 60-day limb of the secondary test is relaxed to 182 days — but only while Indian-source income stays at ₹15 lakh or below. Above that, the relaxation is to 120 days instead. Spend 120 to 181 days in India with 365 days or more across the preceding four years, and you become resident, specifically RNOR.

    Does the ₹15 lakh threshold include my foreign salary?

    No. The test counts income sourced in India only. Salary abroad, interest on foreign deposits and rent from an overseas property are excluded.

    Can I be a tax resident of India without setting foot there?

    Under the deemed residency rule, yes — if you are an Indian citizen, your Indian-source income is above ₹15 lakh, and you are not liable to tax in any other country. It is aimed at citizens in zero-tax jurisdictions. If you pay tax somewhere abroad it does not apply, and even when it does apply the status conferred is RNOR, so foreign income generally stays outside the Indian net.

    How long does RNOR last when I move back?

    Commonly two to three financial years for someone who has been abroad a long time, because the tests look back over 10 years and over 7 years. The exact length depends on your own history, which is why the calculator asks for it.

    Why does RNOR matter so much?

    Because during it your foreign income generally remains outside Indian tax, as it did while you were an NRI, with a narrow exception for a business controlled from India. Once you become ordinarily resident, worldwide income is taxable. The window does not repeat.
