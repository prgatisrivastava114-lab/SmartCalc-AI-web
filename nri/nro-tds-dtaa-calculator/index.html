NRO interest — what the bank takes, and what it could take

Interest on an NRO account is taxed at the full Indian non-resident rate. If you are tax-resident in a country with an Indian treaty, a lower rate may apply — but only if the paperwork reaches the payer before the interest is paid.

About this calculator

An NRO account holds money that arises in India — rent, dividends, pension, sale proceeds. The interest it earns is fully taxable, and the deduction rate for a non-resident is far above the resident rate on the same deposit.

Many NRIs never discover that a treaty may reduce it, because the default behaviour of a bank is to apply the full domestic rate and say nothing. This calculator puts a number on that silence.

How this calculator works

  1. Full rate. 30% plus 4% cess, applied to the interest you enter.
  2. Treaty rate. Whatever rate you enter, applied to the same interest.
  3. The gap. The difference between the two, which is either recoverable by filing a return, or avoidable at source with the right paperwork.

What you need for a treaty rate

To be taxed at a treaty rate instead of the full Indian rate you need a Tax Residency Certificate (TRC) from the tax authority of your country of residence, plus Form 10F filed online on the Indian income tax portal. Both are annual.

Without a TRC and Form 10F the payer must deduct at the full Indian rate. You can still recover the excess by filing an Indian return, but the money is locked up until the refund arrives.

The formula

Full deduction = Interest × 30% × (1 + cess)

Treaty deduction = Interest × your treaty rate

Annual gap = Full deduction − Treaty deduction

A worked example

Suppose ₹8,00,000 of NRO interest in a year, and a treaty rate of 12.5% on interest.

  • Full Indian deduction: ₹8,00,000 × 31.2% = ₹2,49,600
  • At the treaty rate: ₹8,00,000 × 12.5% = ₹1,00,000
  • Difference in that one year: ₹1,49,600

Repeat that across several years of deposits and the paperwork starts to look like the highest-paid hour of admin in the whole arrangement.

Assumptions and limitations

  • The treaty rate is the one you enter. We do not supply per-country rates, because they vary by treaty and by income type and a wrong figure would be worse than none.
  • Surcharge is not modelled. At higher income levels a surcharge may apply on top of the base rate.
  • Treaty benefit depends on eligibility, not merely on residence — some treaties carry conditions.
  • This covers interest. Dividends, capital gains and royalties are treated differently, often under separate treaty articles.
  • Whether you can recover excess deduction by filing depends on your overall Indian income for the year.

Frequently asked questions

Why does the bank deduct so much more from me than from a resident?

Because the deduction rules for payments to non-residents are separate from the resident rules and are set at a higher flat rate. The bank is not making a judgement about you; it is applying the default that applies to non-residents in the absence of a treaty claim.

Can I get the extra back?

Generally yes, by filing an Indian return and claiming credit for what was deducted against what you actually owe. The drawback is timing — the money sits with the department until the refund is processed.

Is it better to claim the treaty rate at source or reclaim later?

At source, almost always. Reclaiming works but costs you the use of the money for months. Lodging a Tax Residency Certificate and Form 10F before interest is paid avoids the problem rather than correcting it.

Does any of this apply to my NRE account?

No. Interest on NRE and FCNR accounts is exempt and no tax is deducted, which is precisely why foreign earnings are usually better held there than in an NRO account.

I live in a country with no income tax. Can I still claim a treaty rate?

It depends on whether your country has a treaty with India and whether its authority will issue you a Tax Residency Certificate. Without a certificate there is no treaty claim. Separately, be aware that Indian citizens with substantial Indian income who are not liable to tax anywhere can be caught by the deemed residency rule.
