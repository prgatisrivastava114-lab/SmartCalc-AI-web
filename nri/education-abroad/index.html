Paying for education across two countries

Most writing on this topic opens with a TCS rate. That is the wrong place to start, because the rule turns on who is paying and where they live — and for a great many NRI families it does not apply at all.

Positions stated as at 2026-11-14 for FY 2026-27 (AY 2027-28). Educational estimates — not tax advice.
यह पेज हिंदी में पढ़ें

Start with who is paying, not what the rate is

Resident individuals remitting money out of India. A non-resident paying a foreign institution from foreign income is outside the scheme entirely.

You live abroad, pay a foreign university from foreign incomeThe scheme does not apply
A parent or grandparent in India sends the feesThe scheme applies to them
You have moved back to India and are funding a child still abroadThe scheme applies to you

This single distinction decides whether anything on the rest of this page is relevant to you. An NRI in Dubai paying a Dubai university from a Dubai salary is not remitting money out of India, so there is nothing for an Indian bank to collect. The moment the money leaves India, it changes.

Why the confusion is expensive in both directions. Families who assume the charge applies to them sometimes restructure payments for no reason. Families who assume it never applies get caught when a relative in India sends a large transfer, or when they move home mid-degree and keep paying fees from an Indian account.

When money does leave India: the rate card

These rates took effect 1 April 2026. They apply to remittances out of India by a person resident in India.

Purpose of the remittanceRateApplies to
Education funded by a loan from a specified financial institutionNilNo threshold — any amount
Education, self-funded2%On the amount above ₹10 lakh
Medical treatment abroad2%On the amount above ₹10 lakh
Overseas tour package2%From the first rupee — no threshold
Investment, gifts, maintenance, everything else20%On the amount above ₹10 lakh

The threshold is cumulative. Cumulative per PAN per financial year, across every bank and every purpose — not per transaction. Two transfers of ₹6 lakh through different banks put you over it just as surely as one of ₹12 lakh.

The single most useful line on this page: TCS is advance tax, not a fee. It is credited against your income tax liability and refundable through your return if it exceeds what you owe. People routinely treat it as a cost of sending money abroad and plan around it. It is not a cost. It is timing.

Note the structural point hiding in the table: education financed by a loan from a specified financial institution sits at nil, with no threshold at all, while the same education funded from savings is charged above the limit. Whether borrowing makes sense for your family is a different question entirely — it depends on the interest rate, your liquidity and your tax position, and it is not a decision this page can make for you.

Planning the fees themselves

The remittance mechanics are the small part. The larger part is that an education bill is a goal like any other: it has a date, a size, and an inflation rate — and for an NRI family the inflation rate depends on where the course is.

QuestionWhy it changes the number
Where will the course be?Fees abroad inflate at that country's rate; an Indian course inflates at India's. Applying one rate to both overstates one of them.
Which currency will you pay in?If you earn in one currency and the fees are in another, the plan contains an exchange-rate assumption whether you state it or not.
When does it start, and for how long?A four-year course is four separate inflated payments, not one. Each year costs more than the last.
Will you still be abroad then?Your residential status at the time affects where the money sits and how it moves.

If you are moving back before the course ends

This is where the two halves of the page meet. While you are non-resident, paying foreign fees from foreign income is outside the scheme. Once you return and become resident, the same payment made from India becomes a remittance out of India, and the rate card above starts to apply.

Residency is tested per financial year, so the year in which you move is the year to look at carefully. Check your residential status →  ·  What changes when you return →

What this page does not tell you

  • Whether to borrow or pay from savings. The nil rate on loan-funded education is a fact; whether a loan suits your family is a judgement involving interest costs and your own tax position.
  • The tax treatment in your country of residence. Some countries tax or restrict money moved for education. This site cannot see those rules.
  • University-specific fee structures. Indian institutions sometimes price differently for students holding foreign passports or applying from abroad. Confirm with the institution.
  • Scholarship or visa implications. Out of scope here and genuinely consequential.

The collection obligation sits in the TCS provisions applying to remittances under the scheme. The Department publishes both the 2025 Act and the 1961 Act, and the section numbering differs between them, so this page describes the rule rather than citing a number.

Frequently asked questions

Do NRIs pay TCS on money sent for education?

Usually not, and the common phrasing of this question hides the reason. The collection applies to remittances made out of India by people resident in India. An NRI living abroad who pays a foreign university from foreign income is not sending money out of India at all, so nothing is collected. It becomes relevant when the payer is resident in India — a relative funding the fees from home, or you yourself after moving back.

What is the TCS rate on education remittances in FY 2026-27?

For a resident remitting out of India: nil where the education is funded by a loan from a specified financial institution, with no threshold at all; and 2% on the amount above the ₹10 lakh annual threshold where the education is self-funded. The threshold is cumulative across every bank and every purpose in the financial year, counted against one PAN.

Is TCS an extra cost on top of the fees?

No. It is advance tax, credited against your income tax liability and refundable through your return if it exceeds what you owe. The real effect is on cash flow and timing, not on the total cost of educating your child. Planning around it as though it were a fee usually produces worse decisions than simply budgeting for the timing.

My parents in India want to help with my child’s fees abroad. What applies?

They are resident in India and the money leaves India, so the scheme applies to them, against their own PAN and their own annual threshold. Whether the transfer is classified as education or as a gift matters, because the rate differs substantially between those purposes. The purpose stated on the paperwork is not a formality.

Should I plan education costs in rupees or in the foreign currency?

In the currency the fees will actually be paid in, inflated at that country’s rate, then converted at an exchange rate you have stated openly. Converting to rupees first and applying Indian inflation is the common shortcut and it distorts the target, because course fees abroad do not rise at Indian inflation.
