CAGR Calculator

CAGR is the single annual rate that would take your starting amount to your ending amount over the holding period, as if it grew smoothly every year. It is the only fair way to compare investments held for different lengths of time. Formula: CAGR = (End ÷ Start)1/years − 1.

Why CAGR, and not average return

If one fund returned 60% over four years and another returned 25% over eighteen months, which did better? Absolute returns cannot answer that. CAGR can, because it reduces both to the same unit — a per-year rate — and in doing so it strips out the flattery that long holding periods give to mediocre investments.

What CAGR is actually measuring

CAGR answers one question: what constant annual rate, compounded, turns the start value into the end value? It is a smoothed figure by construction. An investment that fell 40% and then doubled has the same CAGR as one that rose steadily — the journey is erased, only the destination counts.

The mistake CAGR protects you from

Dividing total return by the number of years gives a "simple average" that is always higher than the true CAGR, and the gap widens with time. A 100% gain over 10 years looks like 10% a year by that method; the real CAGR is 7.18%. Marketing material often quietly uses the first number. If a return is quoted without the word CAGR, ask.

Where CAGR is the wrong tool

CAGR assumes one lump sum in at the start and one amount out at the end. If you invested in instalments — a SIP, for instance — CAGR cannot describe your return, because each instalment was invested for a different length of time. The right measure there is XIRR, which weights every cash flow by how long it was invested. Use our SIP calculator for instalment-based investing.

Volatility hides inside a CAGR

Two investments with identical CAGRs can feel completely different to own. A smooth 10% and a 10% CAGR made of +45%, −30%, +38% years are the same on paper and nothing alike in practice — and the second one is where most people sell at the bottom. CAGR tells you what happened to the money, not whether you could have lived through it.

How this calculator works

FormulaCAGR = ( Ending value ÷ Starting value ) ^ (1 ÷ number of years) − 1

Raising the growth multiple to the power of one divided by the years converts a total multiple into a per-year rate. Multiply by 100 to express it as a percentage. The period can be fractional — eighteen months is 1.5 years — and the formula still holds.

The simple-average method overstates the return by almost ten percentage points a year. That is not a rounding difference — it is the difference between a genuinely excellent investment and an implausible one, and it is why CAGR is the figure regulators and honest fact sheets use.

Limitations — what this tool does not do

  • CAGR describes only the start and end points. It says nothing about the volatility, drawdowns or the path in between.
  • It cannot handle multiple cash flows. For SIPs, top-ups or partial withdrawals, use XIRR instead.
  • It is a historical measure. A past CAGR is not a forecast and should never be extrapolated as an expected return.
  • It ignores tax, exit loads, expense ratios and transaction costs, all of which reduce what you actually keep.
  • It is a nominal figure. Subtract inflation to understand what the money will actually buy.

Frequently asked questions

What is a good CAGR?
It depends entirely on the asset and the risk taken. A fixed deposit returning 7% and an equity fund returning 7% are not comparable achievements — one carried almost no risk and the other carried a great deal. Judge a CAGR against a relevant benchmark and the risk endured, never in isolation.
What is the difference between CAGR and absolute return?
Absolute return is the total percentage gain regardless of time. CAGR converts that into a per-year rate, which is what makes investments of different durations comparable. A 50% absolute return is excellent over two years and poor over fifteen.
Can CAGR be negative?
Yes. If the ending value is lower than the starting value, CAGR is negative and tells you the annual rate at which the investment shrank.
Should I use CAGR for my SIP?
No. A SIP invests at many different dates, so each instalment has its own holding period. CAGR would misstate your return, usually by a wide margin. XIRR is the correct measure for instalment investing.
Does CAGR account for inflation?
No, it is a nominal rate. A 9% CAGR with 6% inflation is roughly 3% of real growth in purchasing power. Our inflation calculator shows what that difference does over long periods.
