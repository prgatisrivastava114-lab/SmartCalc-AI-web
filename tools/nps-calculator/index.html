NPS Calculator

This calculator projects your NPS corpus at retirement, then splits it the way the rules require: up to 60% can be withdrawn as a tax-free lump sum and at least 40% must buy an annuity, whose income is taxable at your slab rate. The headline corpus is not what you get to spend.

What NPS actually pays you at 60

NPS is the cheapest retirement product in India by expense ratio, and the only one offering a deduction beyond the ₹1.5 lakh 80C ceiling. It is also the most misunderstood, because the corpus you see projected is not money you can freely withdraw. The split below is the part most calculators quietly skip.

The 60/40 split is the whole story

At 60 you may withdraw up to 60% of the corpus as a lump sum, and that withdrawal is tax-free. The remaining 40% must be used to buy an annuity from a life insurer, and the pension it pays is taxable as income every year. So a ₹1 crore corpus does not mean ₹1 crore in hand — it means ₹60 lakh in hand and ₹40 lakh converted into a taxable income stream at whatever annuity rate is available on the day.

Tax deductions: what survives in each regime

Under the old regime you can claim your own contributions within the ₹1.5 lakh ceiling under 80CCD(1), plus an additional ₹50,000 under 80CCD(1B) — the only common way to deduct more than ₹1.5 lakh of personal investment. Under the new regime neither of those is available. What survives in both is 80CCD(2), your employer’s contribution, deductible up to 14% of basic plus DA.

Why employer NPS is the best remaining lever

For anyone on the new regime, 80CCD(2) is effectively the last major deduction standing. Asking HR to restructure part of your CTC into an employer NPS contribution converts taxable salary into a deductible contribution without costing the employer more. On a ₹14 lakh basic, 14% is almost ₹2 lakh of income made tax-free — far more than the ₹50,000 that 80CCD(1B) would have given you under the old regime.

The honest trade-off

Money in NPS Tier-1 is locked until 60, the annuity requirement removes your choice at exit, and annuity rates are modest. Against that: very low costs, equity exposure that has historically beaten fixed-income alternatives, and a deduction nothing else offers. It suits a long horizon and a stable income — it is a poor home for money you might need earlier. Compare it with PPF, which is fully tax-free at maturity but caps your contribution and your return.

How this calculator works

FormulaCorpus = C × [ ((1+i)^n − 1) ÷ i ] × (1+i) · Pension = (Corpus × annuity share) × annuity rate ÷ 12

C is your monthly contribution, i the monthly return (annual ÷ 12) and n the number of months to retirement. The trailing (1+i) treats contributions as made at the start of each month. The pension figure assumes the whole annuitised amount buys a level annuity at the rate you enter — real annuity products vary by type, and a pension that rises with inflation starts much lower.

Worked example

A 30-year-old contributing ₹5,000 a month until 60, assuming 10% a year and a 6% annuity rate on the minimum 40%:

StepAmount
Total contributed over 30 years₹18,00,000
Projected corpus at 60₹1,13,96,627
Tax-free lump sum (60%)₹68,37,976
Amount annuitised (40%)₹45,58,651
Monthly pension at 6%₹22,793

Note the shape of this: ₹18 lakh of contributions becoming roughly ₹1.14 crore is almost entirely compounding, which is why starting age matters far more than contribution size. The same ₹5,000 started at 40 instead of 30 produces a corpus closer to ₹38 lakh — a third as much for two-thirds of the contributions.

Limitations — what this tool does not do

  • Returns are assumed constant. NPS is market-linked; actual sequence of returns, especially near retirement, changes the outcome significantly.
  • Annuity rates are not guaranteed years in advance. The rate available when you turn 60 may be materially different from the one you enter.
  • Fund-management and other charges are not deducted, so the corpus is slightly optimistic.
  • The pension shown is nominal. At 6% inflation, ₹22,793 a month in 30 years buys roughly what ₹4,000 buys today — use our inflation calculator to see this in your own numbers.
  • Partial withdrawal rules, Tier-2 accounts and NPS Vatsalya are not modelled.
  • Small corpuses at exit may be fully withdrawable without buying an annuity; thresholds change, so confirm the current limit with your provider.

Frequently asked questions

Is the ₹50,000 NPS deduction available under the new tax regime?
No. Section 80CCD(1B) is an old-regime benefit only. Under the new regime your own NPS contributions get no deduction at all. The employer contribution under 80CCD(2), up to 14% of basic plus DA, remains deductible under both regimes and is the only meaningful NPS tax benefit left for new-regime taxpayers.
How much of my NPS corpus is tax-free?
Up to 60% can be taken as a tax-free lump sum at 60. The remaining 40% must buy an annuity, and the pension income from it is taxable at your slab rate each year.
Is NPS better than PPF?
They solve different problems. PPF is guaranteed, fully tax-free at maturity and capped at ₹1.5 lakh a year. NPS is market-linked with higher expected returns, offers an extra ₹50,000 deduction under the old regime, but locks money until 60 and forces an annuity at exit. Many people hold both — see our PPF calculator for the comparison.
Can I withdraw from NPS before 60?
Only in limited circumstances. Partial withdrawals from Tier-1 are permitted after a minimum period and only for specified purposes such as education, marriage, home purchase or serious illness, and are capped as a share of your own contributions. Full early exit is heavily restricted.
What return should I assume?
That is a judgement, not a fact. NPS funds hold a mix of equity, corporate bonds and government securities, and the equity share is capped by your chosen allocation. A long-horizon assumption in the high single digits to around 10% is common, but no return is guaranteed and we do not promise one. Try a lower figure and see whether your plan still works — that is the useful test.
