Home Loan Eligibility Calculator

Lenders cap your total EMIs at a share of your net monthly income — the FOIR or fixed-obligation-to-income ratio, commonly 40% to 55%. Your eligibility is whatever loan that permitted EMI can service at the offered rate and tenure, after subtracting EMIs you already pay.

How lenders decide what you can borrow

Buyers usually work out what they want to borrow. Lenders work out what you can repay, using a formula that is unglamorous and fairly mechanical. Knowing it before you apply tells you the realistic price bracket, how much deposit you need, and precisely which lever — income, tenure or clearing an existing loan — moves the number most.

FOIR is the number that decides everything

Lenders add up every fixed monthly obligation you have — car loan, personal loan, credit-card minimums, this new EMI — and check it against your net income. Most keep the total between 40% and 55%, with the higher end offered to higher earners because more rupees remain after the EMI. Clearing a small personal loan before applying can raise your home-loan eligibility by several lakh, which is often the single most effective step a borrower can take.

Eligibility is not affordability

The maximum a lender will approve and the amount you should borrow are different numbers. A 50% FOIR leaves half your income for everything else — groceries, school fees, insurance, saving for retirement, and the emergency fund you will need when the boiler and the car fail in the same month. Borrowing the full eligibility is how households end up with an asset and no financial resilience.

The deposit is bigger than the LTV suggests

Lenders typically fund 75% to 90% of the property value, so you bring the rest. But stamp duty, registration, brokerage, legal fees and the first set of furnishings sit entirely outside the loan and commonly add 7% to 10% of the property price. A ₹60 lakh purchase at 80% LTV needs ₹12 lakh of equity plus perhaps ₹5 lakh of costs — closer to ₹17 lakh in cash.

Tenure is a trap worth understanding

Extending the tenure raises your eligibility because it lowers the EMI, and that is exactly why it is offered. The cost is enormous. A ₹50 lakh loan at 8.5% costs about ₹58 lakh in interest over 30 years against roughly ₹36 lakh over 20 — ₹22 lakh for the privilege of a smaller monthly payment. Borrow over the longest tenure if it gets you approved, then prepay aggressively. Our EMI calculator shows the interest difference.

How this calculator works

FormulaPermitted EMI = (Net income × FOIR) − existing EMIs Loan = EMI × [ 1 − (1+i)^−n ] ÷ i

i is the monthly interest rate — the annual rate divided by twelve — and n the number of monthly instalments. The second expression is the present value of your permitted EMI over the tenure, which is precisely what a lender can advance against it. Property budget is then the loan divided by the loan-to-value ratio the lender funds.

The loan figure is the easy part. The ₹16.1 lakh of cash is what decides whether the purchase is possible this year or in three years — and it is the number most buyers discover late. If the same applicant were paying a ₹12,000 car EMI, eligibility would fall to about ₹32,26,000 — a drop of nearly ₹14 lakh from one modest loan.

Limitations — what this tool does not do

  • This is an estimate of what the FOIR method permits, not an offer. Lenders also assess credit score, employment stability, age, property title and valuation.
  • It assumes a fixed rate. Most Indian home loans are floating, so your EMI can change after disbursal.
  • Co-applicant income is not included. Adding an earning spouse usually increases eligibility substantially.
  • Stamp duty, registration, brokerage and legal costs are excluded from the loan and must be funded separately.
  • Self-employed applicants are assessed on declared income over several years, which this tool does not model.
  • Some lenders use net income after statutory deductions, others use a different basis — treat the result as indicative.

Frequently asked questions

How much home loan can I get on a ₹80,000 salary?
On the standard method, roughly ₹45–50 lakh at 8.5% over 20 years with no existing EMIs and a 50% FOIR. The figure moves a great deal with your other obligations, the tenure, the rate offered and whether you add a co-applicant. Enter your own numbers above for a specific estimate.
What is FOIR?
The fixed-obligation-to-income ratio — the share of your net monthly income that lenders allow to go towards all EMIs combined. Most lenders work between 40% and 55%, and higher earners are generally allowed the upper end.
Does a co-applicant increase eligibility?
Usually yes. Adding an earning co-applicant pools the incomes, so the permitted EMI rises. Both become jointly liable for the loan, and both credit histories are assessed.
Should I borrow the maximum I am eligible for?
Rarely. Eligibility is the lender’s risk limit, not your comfort limit. Leaving room for an emergency fund, insurance and retirement saving matters more than buying at the top of your bracket — especially on a floating rate, where the EMI can rise.
Does my credit score affect how much I can borrow?
It affects whether you are approved and at what rate, and the rate directly changes the loan a given EMI can support. A one-percentage-point higher rate reduces eligibility by roughly 8–10% over a 20-year tenure.
